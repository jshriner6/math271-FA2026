    Worksheet Matrices (Complete)
    

    1. Motivation.

    🔗
    Solution.
    Matrices are a foundational tool in linear algebra, and used for representing observables (like energy, momentum) in quantum systems. We’ll establish some necessary basics for working with and describing matrices, and then work towards understanding deeper uses and interpretations of matrices.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Matrix Structure.

    An \(m \times n\) matrix has
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, in which each entry holds a scalar (either a real number or a complex number, depending on context).
    🔗
    Some special matrices are row vectors (\(m=1\)), column vectors (\(n=1\)), and square matrices (\(m=n\)).
    🔗
    It is often convenient to view matrices as a collection of column vectors, written
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \vvec_1 \quad \vvec_2 \cdots \vvec_n \right]\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Matrix Arithmetic.

    (a)

    The sum of two matrices, denoted \(A + B\text{,}\) is the matrix whose entries are the sums of the corresponding entries of \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) Compute \(A +B\) for each pair of matrices below, when possible. If not possible, explain why.
    1. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1&3&-1 \\ -2&2&1 \\ 7&8&-10\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}0&2&-5 \\1&3&-1 \\ -5&2&13\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}0&5 \\ -2&3 \\ 10&4\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle \begin{bmatrix}2&1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle A +B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 5 \amp -6 \\ -1 \amp 5 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 10 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle A + B =\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 8 \\ 1 \amp 8 \\ 11 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. This sum is not defined since \(A\) and \(B\) have different dimensions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The number \(0\) is called an additive identity because \(a + 0 = 0 +a = a\) for any number \(a\text{.}\) Is there an additive identity for \(2 \times 2\) matrices? If so, what is it?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes, it is the matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) called the zero matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    For any number \(a\text{,}\) the number \(-a\) is called an additive inverse of \(a\) because \(a + (-a) = 0\text{.}\) Does the matrix \(\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix}2&1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\) have an additive inverse? If so, what is it?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes, it is the matrix \(\displaystyle \begin{bmatrix}-2& -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    If \(b\) is any real number, the matrix \(bA\) is the matrix obtained by multiplying every entry of \(A\) by the number \(b\text{.}\) This is called scalar multiplication. Compute \(bA\) for each pair \(b,A\) below, if possible. If not possible, explain why.
    1. \(\displaystyle A = \begin{bmatrix}1&3&-1 \\ -2&2&1 \\ 7&8&-10\end{bmatrix}, b= 3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle A = \begin{bmatrix}0&5 \\ -2&3 \\ 10&4\end{bmatrix}, b = -5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle 3A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 9 \amp -3 \\ -6 \amp 6 \amp 3 \\ 21 \amp 24 \amp -30 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle -5A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -25 \\ 10 \amp -15 \\ -50 \amp -20 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    We can always compute scalar multiples of matrices.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Does the set of all real matrices form a vector space over \(\R\text{?}\) Explain why or why not.
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. This set is not closed under addition. However, the set of all \(m \times n\) real matrices does form an \(mn\)-dimensional vector space over \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Matrix Multiplication.

    (a)

    Matrix/Vector:
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\left[\vvec_1 \cdots \vvec_n \right] \cdot \left[\begin{array}{r} c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{array}\right] = c_1\vvec_1 + \cdots + c_n\vvec_n\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Matrix/Matrix:
    🔗
    Solution.
    If \(A =\left[\vvec_1 \cdots \vvec_n \right] \) and \(B = \left[\wvec_1 \cdots \wvec_n \right]\text{,}\) then \(A \cdot B = \left[A\wvec_1 \cdots A\wvec_n \right]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(A \cdot B\) and \(B \cdot A\text{,}\) when possible, for each pair of matrices below. If the product is not possible, explain why.
    1. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -1 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \\ 7 & 8 & 2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 2 & -5 \\1 & 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}0&5 \\ -2&3 \\ 10&4\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 3\\ 3 & 5 &1\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}-1&5 \\ 6&10\end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \begin{equation*} AB = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \amp 9 \amp -5 \\ -3 \amp 4 \amp 5 \\ -2 \amp 42 \amp -49 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      and
      \begin{equation*} BA = \begin{bmatrix} -39 \amp -36 \amp -8 \\ -12 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ -30 \amp -35 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \begin{equation*} AB = \begin{bmatrix} 15 \amp 25 \amp 5 \\ 7 \amp 9 \amp -3 \\ 22 \amp 50 \amp 34 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      and
      \begin{equation*} BA = \begin{bmatrix} 24 \amp 26 \\ 0 \amp 34 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(AB\) is undefined, and
      \begin{equation*} BA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Matrix Multiplication (continued).

    (a)

    Is matrix multiplication commutative?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No! In fact, there are times when one ordering of matrix multiplication is defined and the other ordering is not.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Zero Divisors:
    🔗
    Solution.
    Unlike with real numbers, it is possible to multiply two non-zero matrices and get the zero matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Multiplicative Identity:
    🔗
    Solution.
    The multiplicative identity for square matrices \(I_n\) is the matrix with \(1\) down the main diagonal, and \(0\) everywhere else. For an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) it satisfies \(A\cdot I_n =I_n \cdot A = A \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Other Operations on Matrices.

    (a)

    Matrix Transpose
    🔗
    Solution.
    For an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) \(A^T\) is the \(n \times m\) matrix whose columns are the rows of \(A\text{,}\) and whose rows are the columns of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Trace (square matrices)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\text{tr}(A)\) is the sum of the diagonal entries of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Inverse (square matrices)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The inverse matrix \(A^{-1}\) of \(A\) is the matrix such that \(A^{-1} \cdot A = A \cdot A^{-1} = I_n\text{.}\) Not every square matrix has an inverse matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Determinant (square matrices)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The determinant of a matrix \(A\) is a scalar whose computation depends on the dimension of \(A\text{.}\) For \(2\times 2\) matrices it is computed as \(ad-bc\text{.}\) For matrices of higher dimension, you can use technology to compute it.
    🔗
    Among other things, the determinant tells us when a matrix is invertible: a square matrix \(A\) is invertible exactly when \(\text{det}(A) \neq 0\text{.}\)
